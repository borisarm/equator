How it works

How EquatorSolver is built, where its algorithms come from, and how its answers are tested

Built from scratch

EquatorSolver's mathematics engine was written from scratch in C# on .NET 10. It doesn't wrap another computer algebra system and doesn't use any third-party mathematics library: the parser, exact arithmetic, polynomial algebra, calculus, arbitrary-precision numbers and typesetting are all part of the project.

The Windows app uses WinUI 3 (the Windows App SDK) for its interface and Win2D for drawing. Formulas are laid out by the app's own TeX-style box layout, and plots are drawn with Win2D. Everything runs on your PC: the app has no server and never connects to the internet.

The source code is open under the MIT license: github.com/borisarm/equator.

From what you type to the answer

  1. Parsing. Your input, such as integrate(x^2 sin(x), x), is read by a precedence parser into an expression tree.
  2. Canonical form. Expressions are always kept in a canonical form: sums and products are flattened and sorted, like terms are combined and numbers stay exact (fractions, not decimals). Equal expressions therefore look the same, which keeps every later step simple and reliable.
  3. Evaluation. The kernel evaluates the tree in order and calls the command you asked for. It remembers your variables, your own functions and the numbered results (%3).
  4. Display. The result is turned into typesetting boxes and drawn as a formula. The same result can be copied as plain text, LaTeX or an image.

Where the algorithms come from

The algorithms aren't invented here. They are standard, published methods from computer algebra and numerical analysis, implemented from their descriptions in the literature. The main ones:

  • Factoring polynomials: the Zassenhaus approach. A polynomial is factored modulo a small prime with the Cantor–Zassenhaus algorithm, the factors are lifted with Hensel lifting (to a modulus chosen from the Mignotte bound), and then recombined into true factors, following von zur Gathen and Gerhard, Modern Computer Algebra. Polynomials in several variables are factored through a one-variable image and Hensel lifting with Wang's leading-coefficient trick. Repeated factors are separated first with Yun's square-free decomposition.
  • Greatest common divisors: the GCDHEU heuristic of Char, Geddes and Gonnet.
  • Equations: exact roots in radicals where they exist, including Cardano's formula for cubics (in trigonometric form when all three roots are real); resultants from the Sylvester matrix to eliminate variables in systems.
  • Linear algebra: exact arithmetic over the rationals, with Bareiss fraction-free elimination for determinants and Gaussian elimination for linear systems; LU, QR (Gram–Schmidt), Cholesky and Jordan forms computed exactly; the singular value decomposition with the one-sided Jacobi method.
  • Integrals: a heuristic integrator in the "derivative-divides" tradition: a table of integrals, partial fractions for rational functions, substitution, trigonometric rewriting, integration by parts and quadratic radicals. Every antiderivative is differentiated back and must match the integrand before it is shown.
  • Limits: continuity, L'Hôpital's rule and rewriting of indeterminate forms (0·∞, ∞ − ∞, exponential forms), with a numeric fallback that uses Richardson extrapolation.
  • Sums and series: polynomial sums by Lagrange interpolation; alternating series accelerated with the Cohen–Villegas–Zagier method; ζ(2n) through Bernoulli numbers; Taylor and Laurent expansions.
  • Differential equations: separable and first-order linear equations, and linear equations with constant coefficients of any order through the characteristic roots and variation of parameters.
  • Numerical methods: Durand–Kerner iteration with Newton polishing for all roots of a polynomial; Brent's method for real roots of functions; adaptive Gauss–Kronrod (7–15) and tanh-sinh (double exponential) quadrature for definite integrals; Newton's method for systems.
  • Any precision: the app's own arbitrary-precision floating-point numbers, with π from Machin's formula.
  • Recognising exact constants: the PSLQ integer-relation algorithm of Ferguson and Bailey finds rationals, algebraic numbers and combinations of π, e, logarithms and square roots from high-precision decimals. Every candidate is evaluated again and must agree to nearly all its digits before it is accepted.
  • Assumptions such as x > 0 are tracked with interval arithmetic, which is how the app decides signs and simplifies, for example, sqrt(x^2) to x.
  • Checking your work: each line of a worked solution is compared with the line before it. Equations, systems and inequalities must keep the same solution set, and expressions must stay equal (for indefinite integrals, up to a constant). That's how the checker finds the first wrong step and notices lost or extra solutions.

How the answers are tested

About 4,700 automated tests run on every change. Besides comparing with expected answers, many tests check a result against an independent computation: definite integrals against numerical quadrature, antiderivatives by differentiation, solutions of equations by substitution, factorisations by multiplying back and matrix decompositions by their defining properties. Random expressions are generated to test expanding, factoring, simplifying and differentiating.

About 2,500 of those tests are exercises from the OpenStax algebra textbooks (CC BY 4.0), with each book's own answer: the app solves, factors and simplifies them and its answer is compared with the book's. The few exceptions are listed in the source code, each with its reason.

Limitations

EquatorSolver is a compact computer algebra system. Integration is heuristic (every result is verified, but there is no complete Risch algorithm), so some integrals that have a closed form aren't found. Polynomials of degree four and higher that can't be factored get numeric roots, except for special forms. Equations return principal solutions rather than general families such as x = π/6 + 2kπ; to list every solution in a range, give an interval: solve(2 sin(3t) = 1, t, 0, 2π).
